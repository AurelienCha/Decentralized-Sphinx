\section{Introduction}

A mix network (or mixnet) is an overlay network of servers, called mixnodes, designed to prevent adversaries from correlating senders with receivers~\cite{chaum-mix,cypherpunk-remailer,piotrowska2017loopix,nym-network-whitepaper,danezis2003mixminion, van2015vuvuzela,mixmaster-spec,chaum2016cmix}. 
Unlike Tor~\cite{onion-routing96}, where all packets follow the same path throughout a session (circuit-based), mixnets route each packet independently along different paths (packet-based), making traffic analysis significantly harder.
To further strengthen resistance against traffic analysis, mixnets employ techniques such as packet shuffling, reordering, and fake packets injection, aiming to resist even powerful adversaries capable of observing the entire network. 
One of the most important advancements in mixnet design is the Sphinx packet format, introduced by Danezis et al.~\cite{sphinx} in 2009.
First, Sphinx ensures that all packets are of fixed size and structure, preventing correlation through traffic analysis.
It also employs layered encryption, ensuring that each mixnode learns only its immediate predecessor and successor, nothing more.
Additionally, it includes integrity checks to detect tampering. 
If any part of the routing data is altered, whether accidentally or with malicious intent, the next mixnode will reject the packet.

Sphinx is used in various Anonymous Communication Networks (ACNs), including Nym~\cite{nym-network-whitepaper}, Katzenpost~\cite{infeld2025echomix}, and the Lightning Network~\cite{poon2016bitcoin}, all aiming to preserve unlinkability between packet origin and destination.
Sphinx packets are constructed by the client, who encodes all the routing information such as IP addresses and cryptographic elements within the packet header.
While this design simplifies routing, it opens the door for malicious clients who could bias the routes and not follow the randomized path selection, reducing the overall anonymity provided by the mixnet~\cite{benguirat2022mixnet}. 
Additionally, in systems like Nym, where users must pay a subscription fee to route messages for a specific application, users authenticate using anonymous credentials. 
However, nothing prevents them from using the network to route traffic for an unauthorized application they have not paid for, potentially overloading the network.

In this paper, we present a novel scheme to create Sphinx headers in a decentralized manner using trusted third parties, while ensuring that these parties learn nothing about the route, destination, or cryptographic secrets. 
Having a decentralized scheme offers greater flexibility and robustness for real-world deployment and facilitates ongoing improvements of the technology and its features.  
We argue that a decentralized approach will simplify the issuance of anonymous credentials and enable enforcement of access control policies without compromising privacy.

\noindent Our work makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We propose a decentralized approach to Sphinx header construction;

  \item We provide a Python implementation of our header construction to support a comprehensive evaluation of our approach in Nym or other mixnets;

  \item We evaluate the unlinkability property of our decentralized construction by analyzing the randomness of the headers using the NIST SP 800-22 statistical test suite. 
  We show that our construction passes all the tests that the original Sphinx passed. 
  Additionally it succeeds in three tests where the original Sphinx packet format fails.
\end{itemize}
