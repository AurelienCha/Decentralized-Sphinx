
\section{Introduction}


Privacy at the network layer, such as in mixnet-based systems~\cite{nym, piotrowska2017loopix, infeld2025echomix} for communication and in the Lightning Network for financial transactions, is achieved through layered encrypted packets that traverse multiple nodes, with each node decrypting one layer and forwarding the packet to the next hop. In order to prevent malicious routing nodes as well as network adversaries from de-anonymizing traffic at the network layer, each intermediary node learns only its immediately preceding and succeeding nodes, not its position within the route. Additionally, packets are padded to a fixed size and processed such that their format reveals no linkable information across hops, preventing traffic correlation based on size or format.

At the core of these guarantees is Sphinx~\cite{sphinx}, a compact fixed-size packet format that provides cryptographically strong unlinkability and per-hop integrity protection~\cite{sphinx}. The Sphinx packet format, introduced by Danezis and Goldberg~\cite{sphinx}
in 2009, has become a foundational component of modern anonymous
communication systems such as
the Lightning Network~\cite{poon2016bitcoin}, Nym~\cite{nym} and
Katzenpost~\cite{infeld2025echomix}.

However, despite its strengths, the Sphinx packet format is constructed entirely by the clients before entering the network.  This
centralization makes the system vulnerable to attacks by malicious clients.  For
example, a client may bias route selection by violating the routing
algorithm, thereby reducing the number of messages mixed at a specific node
in order to target an honest client or degrade the system's overall
anonymity~\cite{benguirat2022mixnet}. In the Lightning Network, a malicious client can launch a jamming attack by sending payments through honest nodes and deliberately never resolving them, locking their channels and freezing liquidity. By repeatedly routing through specific nodes and observing response times, the attacker can also infer their current payment load and activity patterns~\cite{Rohrer2019paymentchannels, kappos2021empirical}.


We therefore argue that decentralizing Sphinx header construction is
crucial in achieving more robust, verifiable, and flexible anonymous
communication systems and decentralized networks.

In this work, we introduce DSphinx, a \emph{decentralized Sphinx header
construction} that distributes the computation across multiple independent
semi-trusted third parties (STTPs).
%
Achieving decentralization while maintaining Sphinx's cryptographic
guarantees raises several challenges.  First, the protocol must prevent
individual STTP, or coalition of corrupted STTPs, from learning routing
information or recovering cryptographic secrets.  Second, mixnodes should learn no more than in the traditional
Sphinx design.  Each node must reveal only its own shared secret and the
address of the next node, but nothing about the rest of the route.  Third,
unlinkability must be preserved even against a powerful passive adversary
capable of observing all traffic inputs and outputs and performing
sophisticated cryptanalytic analysis.  Finally, distributing the
computation must not introduce any trapdoor, bias, or structural weakness
that an adversary could exploit to correlate packets or manipulate header
values.

Our construction addresses these challenges by encoding routing information
as elliptic curve points, splitting secrets via additive secret sharing,
and using a homomorphic pseudorandom weighted checksum as an integrity
mechanism that remains compatible with decentralized computation.  To the
best of our knowledge, this is the first work to decentralize the Sphinx
packet format. 
Our decentralized header construction represents a step toward more robust, verifiable, and flexible decentralized networks by enabling distributed path selection, and efficient enforcement of exit policies.
