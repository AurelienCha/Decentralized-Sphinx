\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have proposed a decentralized scheme that delegates the construction of Sphinx headers to a set of Trusted Third Parties (TTPs). 
This design ensures that TTPs cannot infer any information about the route or cryptographic secrets, even when colluding, provided that at least one TTP remains honest.
To evaluate our approach, we developed a Python implementation and performed a series of statistical randomness tests to assess the unlinkability of our Sphinx packets. 
The results demonstrate that our decentralized construction achieves comparable, and in some cases improved, output randomness relative to the original centralized Sphinx design.

Decentralizing the construction process introduces greater flexibility for real-world deployment and long-term evolution of the protocol.
In particular, it opens promising avenues for integrating mechanisms that allow verification of packet legitimacy at any hop. 
Such functionality would enable mixnodes to detect and discard unauthorized traffic early in the path, thereby conserving computational resources and mitigating denial-of-service threats. 
A potential approach involves having TTPs dynamically update credentials during header construction, and allowing mixnodes to further update them during packet processing.
We highlight the development of such legitimacy verification mechanisms as an important and promising direction for future research.

An additional future work toward a fully decentralized scheme would involve delegating route selection to the TTPs themselves.
This could help prevent biased or predictable path choices by clients, thereby enhancing overall anonymity.

Before real-world deployment, a comprehensive security evaluation of our scheme is still requiered. 
This includes formal verification of the protocol’s correctness and privacy properties, as well as a detailed threat analysis to identify potential vulnerabilities in our scheme. 
Additionally, a careful evaluation of the computational overhead introduced by our approach, compared to the resource cost of processing illegitimate traffic, will help to determine the overall trade-off of our design.

In summary, our decentralized design offers a promising advancement toward scalable and privacy-preserving anonymous communication.
By redistributing trust from a single client to a set of collaborative TTPs, our approach enhances flexibility and robustness of anonymity systems.
We view this work as a small step into decentralized cryptographic architectures and hope it contributes to the development of more secure and privacy-preserving communication systems.
