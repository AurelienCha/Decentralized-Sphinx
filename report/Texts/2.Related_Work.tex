\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Decentralized Networks}
Since Chaum's seminal work on untraceable email in 1981~\cite{chaum-mix}, there has been a great amount of research related to mixnet design~\cite{piotrowska2017loopix, van2015vuvuzela, kwon2020xrd, lazar2018karaoke, cottrell1995mixmaster, alexopoulos2017MCMIX, chaum2016cmix, chaum-mix, danezis2003mixminion}.

A mixnet is a network of servers, called mixnodes, organized into sequential layers. 
Each packet traverses a fixed path of mixnodes. 
It enters through an entry node, passes through a set of intermediate nodes, and exits via a final node. 
Several topologies have been proposed throughout the literature. In Figure~\ref{fig:mixnet}, we illustrate a stratified topologie used in the Nym networ~\cite{nym-network-whitepaper}.
Each timestamp (from $t_0$ to $t_6$) represents a point in time, showing how packets traverse the network and are reshuffled across layers.

\subsection{Sphinx packet format}
% Sphinx
The \emph{Sphinx} packet format introduced by Danezis and Goldberg~\cite{sphinx} is a compact, provably secure, layer encrypted packet construction designed specifically for mix networks.  
It provides three essential security properties that enable anonymous communicationca: per-hop confidentiality, per-hop integrity protection, and strong unlinkability.

First, Sphinx ensures \emph{per-hop confidentiality} by encrypting routing information in layers, similar to classical onion routing.  
Each mixnode learns only its immediate predecessor and successor, but gains no knowledge about the overall path, the sender, or the final destination.  
This layered encryption relies on a hop-by-hop elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDH) key exchange, enabling the client to derive a distinct shared secret for every mixnode.

Second, Sphinx provides \emph{per-hop integrity protection} through a cryptographic tag embedded in each layer of the header.  These tags prevent adversaries from tampering with routing information.  
Any modification results in an integrity-tag verification failure at the mixnode, causing the packet to be discarded.
This ensures that adversaries cannot tamper with routes or perform active attacks such as header tagging in order to target specific messages or clients.

Third, Sphinx offers \emph{strong unlinkability} by ensuring that packets entering and leaving a mixnode are computationally indistinguishable.  
This is achieved by using fixed-size headers and payloads and by rerandomizing cryptographic elements at every hop through hop-by-hop ECDH, in addition to the layered encryption.
Each hop transforms the packet into a fresh, uniformly distributed header, preventing an adversary from linking an incoming packet to its outgoing counterpart, even when observing the entire network.

These guarantees have made Sphinx the de facto standard for privacy-preserving routing in modern decentralized systems such as the Lightning Network~\cite{poon2016bitcoin} and mixnet-based architectures like Nym~\cite{nym-network-whitepaper} and Katzenpost~\cite{infeld2025echomix}.  
However, in all these systems, the Sphinx header must be fully constructed by the client, which introduces vulnerabilities to malicious client.
A malicious client can intentionally bias route selection, thereby reducing the anonymity of honest users, or even orchestrate an (N-1)-attacks to trace packets.  

In addition, enforcing policies such as accountability and legitimacy verification remains inherently challenging in Sphinx-based systems, because headers are constructed to reveal minimal information to mixnodes.  
As a result, networks cannot reliably verify client credentials, enforce usage policies, or apply access control before packets reach the exit node.  
This trade-off between strong privacy and limited accountability, coupled with the risks posed by untrusted or misbehaving clients, presents practical obstacles for the widespread deployment of mixnet-based systems.  
Introducing a decentralized header construction among semi-trusted third parties could provide additional flexibility, allowing policy enforcement mechanisms to be integrated without compromising the cryptographic guarantees of Sphinx.

These limitations highlight the need for a mechanism to decentralized header construction while preserving the cryptographic guarantees of Sphinx.
To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has proposed or analyzed a decentralized construction of Sphinx headers, making this a previously unexplored but increasingly necessary design direction for robust and trust-minimized anonymous communication systems.
