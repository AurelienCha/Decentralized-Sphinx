\section{Threat Model and Security Properties}~\label{sec:sys_model}

In this section, we present our threat model, and outline the privacy and security requirements our solution should satisfy.

\subsection{Threat Model}

We propose a decentralized construction of Sphinx headers involving semi-trusted third parties (STTPs). 
Figure~\ref{fig:overall_schema} summarizes our model, which involves three types of entities: the client, a set of semi-trusted third parties (STTPs), and the set of mixnodes constituing the mixnet.

We consider different types of adversaries:
\begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Global Passive Adversary (GPA):} 
        An adversary able to observe the whole network, specifically all the traffic entering and leaving at each mixnode. 
        The GPA aims to break the unlinkability by correlating mixnode's incoming packets with its outgoing packets, along the packet path to discover the sender and the recipient.

        \item \textbf{Global Active Adversary (GAA):} 
        An adversary able to modify or inject packets across the entire network. 
        The GAA attempts to forge integrity tags to alter or create valid Sphinx headers.

        \item \textbf{Honest-but-curious STTPs:} 
        In our decentralized scheme, multiple semi-trusted third parties participate in generating the headers. 
        These STTPs are assumed to be honest-but-curious, meaning they follow the protocol but may collude in order to extract additional information from the data they process.

        \item \textbf{Corrupted mixnode:} % instead of malicious client
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Security \& Privacy Objectives}
\label{sec:sp-objectives}

Our main security and privacy objectives are the following:
\begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Unlinkability:} 
        Our scheme aims to ensure that a global adversary cannot link incoming packets to their corresponding outgoing packets at any mixnode.
        We target a comparable level of unlinkability as the original Sphinx packet introduced by Danezis and Goldberg~\cite{sphinx} which is known to achieve strong unlinkability guarantees.
        % If packets appear uniformly random and indistinguishable from one another, we consider that this prevents correlations between incoming and outgoing packets, thereby satisfying the unlinkability requirement.

        \item \textbf{Integrity forgery resistance:} 
        Original Sphinx packet format include an integrity tags for each mixnode in the path preventing any valid packet modification.
        Our scheme should adapt the integrity tags for a decentralized construction.

        \item \textbf{Collusion Resistance:} 
        Since our scheme introduces semi-trusted third parties (STTPs), compared to the original Sphinx format, we must ensure its resilience when those entities collude.
        Even if a majority of STTPs collude, but not all of them, they must learn nothing about the route, destination or cryptographic secrets.
\end{itemize}

	A mixnet is a network of servers, called mixnodes, organized into sequential layers. 
	Each packet traverses a fixed path of mixnodes. 
	It enters through an entry node, passes through a set of intermediate nodes, and exits via a final node. 
	At each layer, mixnodes shuffle and delay packets, obfuscating the link between incoming and outgoing traffic and improving resistance against global adversaries.
	This process is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mixnet}, which provides a step-by-step tracing of two clients communicating with services through a 3-layer mixnet.

	The packets are labeled by color (client identity) and number (message order), allowing us to visually follow how each message is mixed and relayed before reaching its destination.
	For clarity in both formulas and figures, and without loss of generality, we consider a mixnet with three layers throughout the paper.
	
	This kind of network often relies on privacy-preserving packet format with fixed-size, such as Sphinx~\cite{sphinx}.
	%In this work, we will solely focus on the Sphinx packet format.
	Some privacy-focused companies, like Nym Technologies, commercialize an advanced mixnet services that other applications can access for a fee.
	For example, if Signal is integrated with Nym, clients who want their traffic to be anonymous would route their traffic through the mixnet instead of connecting directly to the Signal server. 
	This prevents adversaries monitoring either the client's device, the server, or any intermediate nodes, from correlating sender and recipient.
	In addition to Sphinx packets, they often improved the system with extra features such as \textit{anonymous credentials}.
	For example, each Nym client receives anonymous credentials (based on Coconut credential~\cite{coconut}) to use the mixnet for routing their traffic towards specific destinations they have paid for.
	A potential vulnerability arises when a client obtains a credential for a specific service but uses it to inject traffic directed to an unauthorized or unrelated service. 
	Because the true destination is only revealed at the final hop of the mixnet, earlier mixnodes waste computational resources processing a packet that will ultimately be dropped. 
	This misuse opens the door to denial-of-service (DoS) attacks, where malicious clients flood the system with unauthorized packets that will be detected at the last hop, draining bandwidth and compute capacity.
	
	
	To address this, we propose a decentralized construction of Sphinx headers involving a set of trusted third parties (TTPs). 
	This decentralized scheme could enable TTPs to update credentials and/or zero-knowledge proofs in accordance with the layered encrypted header, to be verifiable by any mixnodes on the route.  
	As a result, mixnodes could early discard unauthorized traffic before it consumes significant resources.
	However, the credentials issuance, modification, and verification are outside the scope of this paper and are left for future work.
