\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

%Our prototype implementation is available on \href{https://github.com/AurelienCha/Decentralized-Sphinx}{GitHub}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/AurelienCha/Decentralized-Sphinx}}.
Our implementation, which we will release our code under the GPL license,
 is written in Python 3.13 using the \href{https://pypi.org/project/ECPy/}{ECPy}\footnote{\url{https://pypi.org/project/ECPy/}} library for elliptic curve operations. 
ECPy is well-suited for prototyping due to its simplicity, but it is significantly slower than many EC python libraries (approximately one order of magnitude slower). 
However, it is one of the few Python libraries that support Montgomery Curve25519, which is essential for our implementation.

We evaluate our system on two main axes: computational complexity and unlinkability (i.e. resistance to correlation between incoming and outgoing packets).

\subsection{Computational Complexity}

Rather than measuring raw execution time, which depends heavily on hardware
and software, we count the number of cryptographic operations as these are
the computationally most expensive part of the scheme.
%\todo{Can you define \enquote{expensive}?}
Direct comparison with the Sphinx scheme is not straightforward, as cryptographic primitives differ (e.g. Elligator). 
Although such comparison is not entirely meaningful, our protocol is inherently more computationally intensive and will be slower in practice.
Table~\ref{tab:operation_count} counts these operations per party, considering $ m $ TTPs and path of length $ p $. 
To compute the total cost for sending a single message, multiply the cost per TTP by $m$ and the cost per mixnode by $p$.
% CLIENT
Since each TTP constructs its partial header independently, they operate in parallel, minimizing their impact on the overall execution time.
Thus, the number of TTPs mainly affects the client, who performs \bigO{p m} elliptic curve multiplications and additions to split $p$ addresses and cryptographic secrets into $m$ shares.
For each mixnode, the client also executes an Elligator mapping to encode the IP address into an elliptic curve point, a hash and a reverse Elligator mapping (i.e. $Point \rightarrow Integer$) to derive the shared secret (Eq.~\ref{eq:shared_secret}), all scaling linearly with $p$.
% TTP
For each layer, a TTP performs elliptic curve multiplications, additions (Eq.\ref{eq:last_layer}, Eq.\ref{eq:layer_i}) and hashes (Eq.~\ref{eq:integrity}) proportional to the number of chunks.
Since both the number of chunks and layers grow linearly with $p$, the total cost per TTP scales quadratically with $p$.
% MIXNODE
Mixnodes follow a similar pattern, performing EC operations and hashes proportional to the number of chunks (Eq.\ref{eq:integrity_check} and Eq.\ref{eq:decrypt}), thus scaling linearly with $p$.
Each mixnode additionally executes one reverse mapping (Eq.~\ref{eq:derive_secret}) to convert the shared secret point into an integer.

In practice, most mixnet implementations use paths of three to five mixnodes, making \bigO{p} $\approx$ \bigO{1}.
Accordingly, the overall computational burden is more sensitive to the number of TTPs $m$ than to the path length $p$.
As our design remains secure as long as one TTP is honest, only a small number $m$ may be sufficient.
Determining the precise number needed to meet a desired trust threshold is left for future work.


\subsection{Unlinkability Evaluation}
Quantifying unlinkability remains a fundamental challenge in anonymous communication systems.
In Loopix~\cite{piotrowska2017loopix}, the authors introduce a probabilistic metric called the \textit{expected difference in likelihood} to evaluate \textit{sender-receiver third-party unlinkability}.
However, this approach relies on network simulations and specifically assesses the likelihood that a given packet originated from a particular client.
In contrast, our focus is on unlinkability at the mixnode level, meaning the difficulty for a Global Passive Adversary (GPA) to correlate an incoming packet with its corresponding outgoing packet through cryptanalysis.
Therefore, assuming that greater randomness hinders correlation, we evaluate the statistical randomness of packet headers to approximate this form of unlinkability.

\subsubsection{Test Suite Overview}
We rely on the NIST SP 800-22 statistical test suite \cite{NIST-SP80022}, which consists of 15 tests designed to evaluate the quality of random number generators. 
Each test produces a p-value representing how likely the data could be produced by a uniform source. 
If the data are truly random, the distribution of p-values across many samples must follow a uniform distribution.
To support the interpretation of our results, we first provide a brief overview of the specific NIST SP 800-22 tests that are most relevant to our analysis 
(i.e. \textit{Frequency}, \textit{Matching}, \textit{Random Walk} tests).

The \textit{Frequency} test evaluates the proportion of zeros and ones across the entire sequence. 
It determines whether the number of ones and zeros is approximately equal, as expected in a truly random sequence. 

The \textit{Matching} tests examine how often predefined m-bit binary patterns appear within the sequence (by default $ m = 9 $). 
These tests aim to detect excessive repetitions of specific aperiodic patterns that could indicate some hidden structure.
The \textit{Overlapping} version uses a sliding window of $ m $ bits which is shifted from a single bit at each step, allowing overlapping matches.
The \textit{Non-overlapping} version shifts the sliding window from $ m $ bits in case of matching, avoiding overlapping matches.

The \textit{Random Walk} tests transform a binary sequence of 1s and 0s into a sequence of $+1$ (step up) and $-1$ (step down) values. 
A truly random walk is expected to oscillate around zero.
The simplest of these tests is the \textit{Cumulative Sums}, which iteratively computes the cumulative sums of these steps and evaluates the maximum deviation from zero compared to the expected behavior of a truly random walk.
The \textit{Random Excursions} test counts how often each state is visited within each cycle\footnote{A cycle is a segment of the walk between two successive zero states, i.e., states where the cumulative sum equals $0$.} 
and compares this distribution with the theoretical expectations for a random walk.
The \textit{Random Excursions Variant} counts how often each state is visited over the entire walk, rather than cycle by cycle.

In summary, the \textit{Frequency} test evaluates the overall balance between zeros and ones, 
while the \textit{Random Walk} tests bring additional information about their distribution,
and finally the \textit{Matching} tests look for excessive repetition of substrings.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Methodology}
We compare the statistical results (with default parameters) of our implementation with those of the original Sphinx implementation by Danezis\footnote{\href{https://github.com/UCL-InfoSec/sphinx}{https://github.com/UCL-InfoSec/sphinx}}.
All graphs presented in this section correspond to the results of the NIST statistical test suite applied to 20 distinct datasets.
Each dataset is generated under a different setup configuration to assess the variability and robustness of our implementation.
To generate a dataset, we simulate a randomized network of 20 mixnodes and 3 trusted third parties (TTPs). 
For each iteration, a random destination IP address and a path consisting of 3 mixnodes are selected. 
The client then generates shares and distributes them to the three TTPs, which independently compute partial headers. 
These headers are then aggregated and forwarded through the simulated mixnet.
According to the NIST documentation, the statistical test suite requires input sequences of $1\,000\,000$ bits. 
To meet this requirement, generated headers are concatenated until the total length reaches $1\,000\,000$ bits. 
Each dataset contains 100 of those concatenated sequences.

NIST outlines two approaches for interpreting the empirical results of its statistical test suite.
The first evaluates the proportion of sequences that pass each statistical test, as shown in Fig.\ref{fig:proportion}.
The second evaluates whether the distribution of p-values is statistically uniform, as would be expected from random sequences.
These results are presented in Fig.\ref{fig:chisquare}.

\subsubsection{Passing Rate}
The proportion of sequences that successfully pass each statistical test, for both our implementation and the original Sphinx implementation, is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:proportion}. 
Following the NIST guidelines, the significance level is set to $ \alpha = 0.01 $, and the confidence interval over $ m $ samples is defined as:
$$
(1 - \alpha) \pm 3 \sqrt{\frac{\alpha (1 - \alpha)}{m}},
$$
Our implementation fails only three tests: \textit{Non-overlapping Matching}, \textit{Random Excursions}, and \textit{Random Excursions Variant}, all of which are also failed by the original Sphinx implementation.  
In contrast, our implementation successfully passes three tests that the original implementation fails: \textit{Frequency}, \textit{Cumulative Sums}, and \textit{Overlapping Matching}.

\subsubsection{P-Value Distribution}
To evaluate the statistical randomness of headers, we examine the distribution of p-values produced by each NIST SP 800-22 test (Figure~\ref{fig:hist_p-values}).
A Chi-square goodness-of-fit test is applied on each of these histograms to assess their uniformity.
The results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:chisquare}, with a significance threshold of $0.0001$ as recommended by the NIST guidelines.
Two Chi-square results stand out: the \textit{Frequency} and \textit{Cumulative Sums} tests.  
Both were successful for our implementation, and failing for the original Sphinx implementation.

\subsection{Discussion}

In terms of computational complexity, our implementation is slower than the original Sphinx implementation.
However, most of the additional computational workload is distributed among the TTPs, which operate in parallel. 
Since mixnets inherently trade latency for privacy, this computational overhead may be acceptable in practical deployments.


Regarding unlinkability (i.e. output randomness), our decentralized implementation performs better than the original implementation, as it passes a greater number of statistical tests, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:test_summary}.
The results suggest that both implementations may exhibit subtle patterns or hidden structure, as indicated by failures in the \textit{Random Excursions} and the  \textit{Non-overlapping Matching} tests. 
This behavior could potentially come from the underlying mathematical framework (e.g. elliptic curves), which is not inherently truly random.
Nevertheless, our implementation demonstrates a better overall balance between ones and zeros, as reflected in the \textit{Frequency} and \textit{Cumulative Sums} test results.
Given the established security of the original Sphinx protocol, and considering the similarities in core structure, our results indicate that our scheme is a solid starting point toward achieving similar privacy guarantees.
However, we emphasize that a thorough formal security analysis remains necessary to fully assess the protocol's security, which we leave for future work.

At this stage, our decentralized scheme successfully achieves three key
security and privacy objectives as outlined in~\ref{sec:sp-objectives}.
First, it maintains strong unlinkability, comparable to the original Sphinx.
Second, integrity protection is verified at each mixnode, ensuring that message tampering can be reliably detected.
Third, our scheme offers robustness against collusion among TTPs, as long as at least one remains honest, preventing them from learning any information about routing information or cryptographic secrets.
Since credential issuance, modification, and verification are left for future work, the verifiability of legitimate traffic at each mixnode has not yet been achieved.
However, a promising approach involves having TTPs update credentials during header construction, with mixnodes performing further updates during packet processing.


While our scheme introduces higher computational complexity compared to the original Sphinx, it maintains a reasonable polynomial bound, specifically a quadratic complexity.
This makes it viable for real-world applications, especially considering the additional features it could enable.
The decentralized design could simplify future implementation of advanced features, such as a mechanism to verify the legitimacy of traffic at any mixnode.
